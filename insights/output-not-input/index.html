ACADEMIC COMMUNICATION

Output, not input: why medical English is a speaking skill

November 2026 · Academic Communication

Many students study science. Few can explain it confidently in English. That gap is where most oral entrance exams are decided.

Knowledge is not the same as explanation

Conventional preparation concentrates on input: memorising, reading, solving problems. Medical-school interviews, academic debate, research presentations and, later, the clinical workplace depend on output: organising your thinking, expressing it clearly in English and communicating with confidence, in real time.

A student can know every step of glycolysis and still freeze when an examiner asks, "Why does the cell need it?" The knowledge is there. The structure to say it is not.

Two frameworks we use out loud

In MedBridge, students practise two simple structures until they become automatic:

  • PREP for opinion and experience questions: Point → Reason → Example → Point.
  • D · E · E for concept questions: Definition → Explanation → Example.

In MedPath, the same 50 biology and chemistry lessons are taken three levels deeper: Define, Explain, Justify. The topics stay the same; only the depth of the questions changes, until a student can defend an answer under follow-up questions, as in the oral exam.

Every session ends with a record

After each 1:1 session, the instructor writes an LLR (Lesson Learned Report). Brief feedback is given during class; the detailed corrections go into the LLR, so error patterns can be tracked and corrected over time. Every answer is assessed on four criteria: pronunciation, fluency, vocabulary and delivery.

Why it lasts

English memorised for an exam fades. The ability to think and express yourself stays with you, through medical school, into research and across a career in medicine. That is the idea behind the name LYVON: Live On.
